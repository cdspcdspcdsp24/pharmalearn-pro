Metabolism

Enzymatic chemical conversion of drugs, mainly in liver, into metabolites.

Pharmacokinetics, ADME, BiotransformationEnglish + TanglishExam focused

First Understand

What is topic? Enzymatic chemical conversion of drugs, mainly in liver, into metabolites.

Why important? Metabolism changes activity, toxicity, elimination, half-life, drug interactions, and dose requirements.

Clinical applications: Predicting CYP interactions, prodrug activation, dose adjustment in liver disease, avoiding toxic metabolites, and individualizing therapy by genetics.

Every Heading and Subheading

Phase I Reactions

Simple English: Oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis introduce or expose functional groups. CYP450 enzymes are important.

Tanglish: Phase I-la drug-la small chemical change pannuvanga; CYP enzymes common.

Real-life example: Diazepam undergoes oxidative metabolism.

Memory trick: Phase I = introduce handle.

Phase II Reactions

Simple English: Conjugation attaches polar groups such as glucuronic acid, sulfate, acetyl, methyl, or glutathione.

Tanglish: Phase II-la drug-kooda water soluble group attach pannuvanga.

Real-life example: Morphine forms glucuronide metabolites.

Memory trick: Phase II = join and make water-friendly.

First-Pass Effect

Simple English: Gut wall and liver metabolism before systemic circulation lowers oral bioavailability.

Tanglish: Oral drug liver first pass-la reduce aagum.

Real-life example: Propranolol has significant first-pass metabolism.

Memory trick: First-pass steals before systemic entry.

Enzyme Induction

Simple English: Some drugs increase enzyme synthesis, causing faster metabolism of themselves or other drugs.

Tanglish: Sila drugs enzyme count increase pannum; appo metabolism speed aagum.

Real-life example: Rifampicin reduces oral contraceptive effectiveness.

Memory trick: Induction = enzyme increase.

Enzyme Inhibition

Simple English: Some drugs block enzymes, increasing concentration of substrate drugs.

Tanglish: Inhibition-na enzyme work slow; drug level increase.

Real-life example: Macrolides can increase theophylline levels.

Memory trick: Inhibition = interaction danger.

Genetic Polymorphism

Simple English: Inherited enzyme differences make patients poor, intermediate, extensive, or ultra-rapid metabolizers.

Tanglish: Genetics nala metabolism speed person-to-person change aagum.

Real-life example: CYP2D6 ultra-rapid codeine metabolism may increase morphine toxicity.

Memory trick: Genes guide drug speed.

Formula Explanations and Solved Problems

Michaelis-Menten rate

Rate = (Vmax x C) / (Km + C)

Why used: Describe saturable metabolism.

  • Vmax: maximum metabolic rate
  • C: drug concentration
  • Km: concentration at half Vmax

How to remember: identify output first, then place exposure/concentration/time terms around it.

Step example: Vmax 100 mg/h, Km 10 mg/L, C 10 mg/L. Rate=100x10/(10+10)=50 mg/h.

Intrinsic clearance

CLint = Vmax / Km

Why used: Compare metabolic ability of enzyme systems.

  • CLint: enzyme capacity when concentration is low
  • Vmax: maximum rate
  • Km: affinity constant

How to remember: identify output first, then place exposure/concentration/time terms around it.

Step example: Vmax 80, Km 20. CLint=4 L/h.

Hepatic extraction ratio

E = (Cin - Cout) / Cin

Why used: Classify high and low extraction drugs.

  • Cin: drug concentration entering liver
  • Cout: leaving liver

How to remember: identify output first, then place exposure/concentration/time terms around it.

Step example: Cin 100, Cout 30. E=70/100=0.7 high extraction.

Graph Explanations

Michaelis-Menten curve

X-axis concentration; Y-axis metabolic rate. It rises then plateaus at Vmax.

X-axis: Time or concentration depending on graph. Y-axis: concentration, rate, percent dissolved, or cumulative amount.

How exams ask: Exam asks to mark Km at half Vmax and explain saturation.

First-order vs zero-order metabolism

X-axis time; Y-axis concentration. First-order declines exponentially; zero-order declines linearly.

X-axis: Time or concentration depending on graph. Y-axis: concentration, rate, percent dissolved, or cumulative amount.

How exams ask: Exam asks examples: phenytoin and ethanol can show capacity-limited behavior.
