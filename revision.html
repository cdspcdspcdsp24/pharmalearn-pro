Absorption

One-line: Movement of drug from the site of administration into systemic circulation.

Must formula: F = (AUC oral / AUC IV) x (Dose IV / Dose oral)

  • Routes and Site of Absorption: Oral drugs mainly absorb through the small intestine because surface area and blood flow are high. Sublingual and rectal routes may avoid part of first-pass metabolism.
  • Cell Membrane Transport: Drugs cross membranes by passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport, filtration, pinocytosis, or ion-pair transport.
  • Factors Affecting Absorption: Lipid solubility, ionization, pH, particle size, dissolution, gastric emptying, intestinal motility, food, blood flow, dosage form, and drug interactions change absorption.
  • Bioavailability: Bioavailability is the fraction of administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged.
  • First-Pass Metabolism: Drug absorbed from the gut enters portal blood and liver before systemic circulation, so some drug is metabolized before it can act.

Distribution

One-line: Reversible transfer of drug between blood and body tissues after absorption or IV entry.

Must formula: Vd = Amount of drug in body / Plasma concentration

  • Apparent Volume of Distribution: Vd relates amount of drug in body to plasma concentration; it is apparent, not a real anatomical volume.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Albumin binds acidic drugs; alpha-1 acid glycoprotein binds basic drugs. Only free drug usually distributes, acts, and clears.
  • Tissue Binding: Some drugs bind to fat, bone, muscle, or melanin and create reservoirs.
  • Blood-Brain and Placental Barriers: Tight junctions and transporters restrict drug entry into brain; placenta allows many lipid-soluble drugs to cross.
  • Redistribution: Highly lipid-soluble drugs may first enter brain, then move to muscle and fat, ending effect before elimination is complete.

Metabolism

One-line: Enzymatic chemical conversion of drugs, mainly in liver, into metabolites.

Must formula: Rate = (Vmax x C) / (Km + C)

  • Phase I Reactions: Oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis introduce or expose functional groups. CYP450 enzymes are important.
  • Phase II Reactions: Conjugation attaches polar groups such as glucuronic acid, sulfate, acetyl, methyl, or glutathione.
  • First-Pass Effect: Gut wall and liver metabolism before systemic circulation lowers oral bioavailability.
  • Enzyme Induction: Some drugs increase enzyme synthesis, causing faster metabolism of themselves or other drugs.
  • Enzyme Inhibition: Some drugs block enzymes, increasing concentration of substrate drugs.

Excretion

One-line: Removal of unchanged drug and metabolites from the body, mainly by kidney and bile.

Must formula: CLr = Ae / AUC

  • Renal Filtration: Free unbound drug filters through glomerulus into urine.
  • Tubular Secretion: Transporters actively move drugs from blood into tubular fluid.
  • Tubular Reabsorption: Unionized lipid-soluble drug can diffuse back from urine to blood.
  • Biliary Excretion: Liver secretes some drugs/metabolites into bile and intestine.
  • Clearance and Half-Life: Clearance is volume of plasma cleared per time; half-life is time for concentration to fall by 50 percent.

Intravenous Bolus Injection

One-line: Rapid injection of an IV dose directly into systemic circulation.

Must formula: C = C0 x e^(-k t)

  • One-Compartment Assumption: After mixing, the body behaves like one uniform compartment and elimination follows first-order kinetics.
  • Initial Concentration C0: C0 is concentration immediately after IV bolus and complete mixing; it is estimated by extrapolating the elimination line to time zero.
  • Elimination Rate Constant: k is fraction of drug removed per unit time in first-order elimination.
  • Semilog Plot: Log concentration vs time becomes a straight line for first-order elimination.

Intravenous Infusion and Extravascular Administration

One-line: Drug input by constant IV infusion or by absorption from extravascular routes such as oral or IM.

Must formula: Css = R0 / CL

  • Constant Rate Infusion: Drug enters at a constant rate while elimination removes drug. Concentration rises toward steady state.
  • Steady State Css: At steady state, rate of input equals rate of output.
  • Loading Dose with Infusion: Loading dose can rapidly reach target concentration while infusion maintains it.
  • Post-Infusion Decline: After stopping infusion, concentration falls by first-order elimination.
  • Extravascular Absorption: After oral/IM/SC dose, concentration rises by absorption and falls by elimination.

Multi-Compartment Models

One-line: Models where drug moves between central and peripheral compartments with different rates.

Must formula: C = A x e^(-alpha t) + B x e^(-beta t)

  • Central Compartment: Blood and highly perfused organs where drug appears quickly.
  • Peripheral Compartment: Less perfused tissues where drug distributes more slowly.
  • Two-Compartment Curve: Plasma concentration declines in two phases: alpha distribution phase and beta elimination phase.
  • Micro and Macro Constants: Microconstants describe transfer between compartments; macroconstants alpha and beta describe observed curve slopes.
  • Method of Residuals: Also called feathering; separates biexponential curve into distribution and elimination components.
