Absorption

Movement of drug from the site of administration into systemic circulation.

Biopharmaceutics and Pharmacokinetics, ADMEEnglish + TanglishExam focused

First Understand

What is topic? Movement of drug from the site of administration into systemic circulation.

Why important? Absorption controls onset of action, peak effect, oral bioavailability, dose design, and dosage form performance.

Clinical applications: Choosing oral vs sublingual vs transdermal route, managing food interactions, designing sustained-release products, and predicting failed therapy when a patient has vomiting, diarrhea, malabsorption, or altered gastric pH.

Every Heading and Subheading

Routes and Site of Absorption

Simple English: Oral drugs mainly absorb through the small intestine because surface area and blood flow are high. Sublingual and rectal routes may avoid part of first-pass metabolism.

Tanglish: Tablet small intestine-la mostly absorb aagum. Surface area perusa irukkaradhu nala absorption nalla nadakkum.

Real-life example: Paracetamol tablet swallowing-ku after intestine-la dissolve aagi blood-ku pogum.

Memory trick: Small intestine = big surface = big absorption.

Cell Membrane Transport

Simple English: Drugs cross membranes by passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport, filtration, pinocytosis, or ion-pair transport.

Tanglish: Drug membrane cross panna different ways irukku: passive-aa pogalam, carrier help use pannalam, energy use pannalam.

Real-life example: Levodopa uses amino acid transporters to enter the body and brain.

Memory trick: Passive goes with gradient; active uses ATP.

Factors Affecting Absorption

Simple English: Lipid solubility, ionization, pH, particle size, dissolution, gastric emptying, intestinal motility, food, blood flow, dosage form, and drug interactions change absorption.

Tanglish: Drug fat soluble-aa, ionized-aa, stomach emptying speed, food irukka illaya nu absorption change aagum.

Real-life example: Antacids reduce absorption of some acidic/chelating drugs.

Memory trick: Absorption = drug + body + dosage form.

Bioavailability

Simple English: Bioavailability is the fraction of administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged.

Tanglish: Kudutha dose-la evlo amount unchanged-aa blood-ku reach aagudho adhu bioavailability.

Real-life example: IV route has 100 percent bioavailability; oral route may be less because of first-pass metabolism.

Memory trick: F means fraction reaching final blood.

First-Pass Metabolism

Simple English: Drug absorbed from the gut enters portal blood and liver before systemic circulation, so some drug is metabolized before it can act.

Tanglish: Oral drug first liver-ku pogum; liver konjam drug-ai break pannum. Appo available amount kammi aagum.

Real-life example: Nitroglycerin is given sublingually to avoid first-pass metabolism.

Memory trick: First pass = liver checkpoint before main blood.

Dissolution and Dosage Form

Simple English: For many oral solid drugs, dissolution is the rate-limiting step before absorption.

Tanglish: Tablet dissolve aagave illa na absorb aaga mudiyadhu.

Real-life example: Micronized griseofulvin absorbs better than large-particle form.

Memory trick: No dissolve, no absorb.

Formula Explanations and Solved Problems

Absolute bioavailability

F = (AUC oral / AUC IV) x (Dose IV / Dose oral)

Why used: Compare oral product with IV reference.

  • F: fraction reaching systemic circulation
  • AUC oral: exposure after oral dose
  • AUC IV: exposure after IV dose

How to remember: identify output first, then place exposure/concentration/time terms around it.

Step example: Oral AUC 40 mg.h/L after 200 mg; IV AUC 100 mg.h/L after 100 mg. F=(40/100)x(100/200)=0.20=20%.

Relative bioavailability

Frel = (AUC test / AUC standard) x (Dose standard / Dose test)

Why used: Compare two oral dosage forms.

  • Frel: test product availability compared with standard
  • AUC: total exposure

How to remember: identify output first, then place exposure/concentration/time terms around it.

Step example: Test AUC 90, standard AUC 100, same dose. Frel=90/100=90%.

Fick law

Rate = (D x A x K x Delta C) / h

Why used: Explain passive diffusion across biological membranes.

  • D: diffusion coefficient
  • A: surface area
  • K: partition coefficient
  • Delta C: concentration difference
  • h: membrane thickness

How to remember: identify output first, then place exposure/concentration/time terms around it.

Step example: If surface area doubles, rate doubles when other factors are constant.

Graph Explanations

Plasma concentration after oral dose

X-axis is time; Y-axis is plasma drug concentration. Curve rises during absorption, reaches Cmax at Tmax, then falls during elimination.

X-axis: Time or concentration depending on graph. Y-axis: concentration, rate, percent dissolved, or cumulative amount.

How exams ask: Exam asks: mark Cmax, Tmax, AUC, absorption phase, elimination phase.

Dissolution profile

X-axis is time; Y-axis is percent drug dissolved. Faster dissolution usually supports faster absorption.

X-axis: Time or concentration depending on graph. Y-axis: concentration, rate, percent dissolved, or cumulative amount.

How exams ask: Exam asks comparison of immediate-release and sustained-release curves.
